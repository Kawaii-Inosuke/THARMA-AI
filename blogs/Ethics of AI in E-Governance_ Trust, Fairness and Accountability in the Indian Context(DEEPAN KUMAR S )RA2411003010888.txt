Ethics of AI in E-Governance: Trust, 
Fairness and Accountability in the Indian 
Context 
Introduction 
Artificial Intelligence is no longer futuristic—it already influences who receives loans, welfare 
benefits, scholarships, and even what information citizens consume daily. In India, AI-driven 
e-governance promises speed, scale, and efficiency across public services. However, this 
transformation raises critical ethical questions: Who controls the algorithms? Whose data is 
being used? Who is accountable when systems fail? 

The National Workshop on “Ethics of AI in E-Governance: Trust, Fairness and Accountability 
over Time” highlighted that AI in governance is not merely a technical issue but a deeply political 
and ethical one. The discussion emphasized that India’s AI future must be built around 
transparency, human oversight, and institutional accountability. 

AI as a Powerful but Potentially Dangerous Tool 
Air Marshal M. Matheswaran described AI as an extraordinarily powerful instrument that must 
remain subordinate to human judgment. Without ethical safeguards, AI-enabled governance can 
drift toward surveillance and authoritarian control. 

Global examples illustrate this danger. China’s large-scale surveillance ecosystem 
demonstrates how AI can centralize state power. Israel’s AI-based targeting system “Lavender” 
shows how algorithmic tools can influence life-and-death decisions. These cases reveal that 
efficiency can quickly turn into coercion when oversight is weak. 

Yet AI is not inherently harmful. When properly governed, it can enhance research, optimize 
public administration, improve grievance redressal, and strengthen service delivery. The key lies 
in: 

●​ Keeping humans in the decision loop 
●​ Establishing strong legal frameworks 
●​ Ensuring independent audits and oversight 

Without these guardrails, technological capability may outpace democratic control. 

 



The “Invisible Bureaucrat”: Algorithmic Decision-Making 
Journalist Nagraj Nagabushanam introduced the metaphor of AI as the “invisible bureaucrat.” In 
traditional governance, citizens interacted with identifiable officials. Today, algorithmic systems 
silently process applications and determine eligibility. This invisibility creates a new 
accountability vacuum. 

Unlike human officers, algorithms cannot be questioned face-to-face. Citizens often do not even 
know that an AI system is involved in decisions affecting them. This undermines procedural 
fairness and the right to contest decisions. 

He also warned about “dashboard governance”—where performance is measured purely 
through metrics. When systems optimize for numbers rather than social outcomes: 

●​ Welfare agents may rush cases to meet targets 
●​ Citizens may be reduced to data points 
●​ Public service goals may be distorted 

To counter this, he proposed: 

●​ Mandatory disclosure when AI is used 
●​ Clear linkage between metrics and real social outcomes 
●​ Identifiable human accountability 
●​ Periodic review and sunset clauses for AI systems 

AI systems change over time; governance must adapt accordingly. 

Rethinking “Trustworthy AI” from the Global South 
Dr. Papia Sengupta critically examined the popular phrase “trustworthy AI.” Trust, she argued, is 
a human and institutional relationship—not a property of machines. Algorithms do not possess 
morality; they operate within structures of power. 

A major concern is “algorithmic colonialism.” Countries in the Global South generate massive 
volumes of data through digital platforms. However, ownership of AI infrastructure, profits, and 
decision-making power largely resides in the Global North. Data flows outward; control does 
not. 

This imbalance produces risks such as: 

●​ Biased credit scoring systems 
●​ Discriminatory policing algorithms 
●​ Opaque visa profiling mechanisms 
●​ Exploitative data labeling labor markets 



Thus, ethical AI must move beyond corporate slogans. It must center: 

●​ Human rights 
●​ Privacy protection 
●​ The right to explanation 
●​ The right to contest automated decisions 

Technology is never neutral—it reflects the values and power of its creators. 

AI and India’s E-Governance Infrastructure 
Dr. Neela Ganguli connected AI ethics to India’s broader e-governance framework. India’s digital 
public infrastructure—Aadhaar, Jan Dhan, and UPI—has enabled unprecedented scale in 
service delivery. Direct benefit transfers and digital identification have increased efficiency. 

However, systemic failures reveal ethical cracks: 

●​ Biometric mismatches excluding pensioners 
●​ Connectivity barriers affecting rural citizens 
●​ Rigid database categories excluding vulnerable groups 
●​ Design flaws creating “invisible” discrimination 

Even small data-entry decisions—how caste, migration status, or education background is 
recorded—can determine access to benefits. Algorithmic governance magnifies such structural 
inequalities. 

Responsible AI in India must therefore include: 

●​ Fairness audits of datasets and models 
●​ Independent third-party review 
●​ Strong data-protection frameworks 
●​ Citizen-centric design principles 

Efficiency cannot come at the cost of dignity. 

Conclusion: Ethics as a Foundational Principle 
AI in governance is not optional—it is already embedded in public life. The challenge is not 
whether to use AI, but how to govern it responsibly. 

The workshop converged on a few core principles: 

1.​ Transparency about AI use 
2.​ Meaningful mechanisms to contest decisions 
3.​ Human accountability for algorithmic outcomes 



4.​ Periodic evaluation and revision of systems 

For India, with its vast population and deep social inequalities, ethical AI governance is not a 
luxury—it is a democratic necessity. AI should not be treated as technological destiny but as a 
set of choices reflecting societal values. 

If India succeeds, it can model an approach where efficiency and equity reinforce rather than 
undermine each other. If it fails, automation may deepen inequality and erode trust in public 
institutions. 

The ethical future of AI in e-governance depends not on the sophistication of algorithms, but on 
the strength of democratic accountability.
